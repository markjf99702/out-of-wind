      How to flatten a board

      Swipe along the board to take a stroke. The blade cuts under your finger, pushing away or pulling toward you. Dragging back lifts the iron.

      A plane only cuts where the wood stands high under its sole. The jointer is long, so it rides over hollows and takes the high spots down until the face is true. The smoother is short and follows the face. The jack’s curved edge takes deep scoops off the high spots fast.

      Work across the board. A plane can’t cut deeper than the wood either side of its blade, so take it lane by lane, and let it hang off the edge to reach the last strip.

      Pencil. Scribble across the face, then plane. The pencil goes first from the high spots. When a light pass takes it off everywhere, the face is flat.

      Straightedge. Drag along or across the board to lay it down. The side view shows light under it wherever the face dips away.

      Winding sticks. Two sticks across the ends. Sighting down them shows twist; when they line up, the face is out of wind.

      Read the grain on the edge. The lines slope down the way to plane. Go the other way and the grain tears out. A finely set smoother gets away with it.

      The gauge line. The dashed line in the side view is three-quarters of an inch up from the underside. Go through it anywhere and the board’s too thin for the tabletop.

      Grading. A board is done when it’s flat and planed all over. Then it gets a mark each for thickness kept, no tear-out, and strokes within par. All of them is FAS, the top grade of lumber; then Select, No. 1 Common and No. 2 Common.
